Linear Interpolation

The Math Behind Estimating Between Data Points

What it means to estimate a value between two known points, where the formula comes from, and why that estimate stops being trustworthy once you step outside the data.

What interpolation is

Say you know a quantity y at two points, x1 and x2, but not at some x in between. Interpolation is the act of estimating that missing value using only the neighbors you already have. It makes no claim about the true underlying process — it's a best guess built from an assumption about how y behaves between the points you do know.

The straight-line assumption

The simplest assumption is that y changes at a constant rate between x1 and x2 — in other words, that a straight line connects (x1, y1) and (x2, y2). That's linear interpolation. Once you accept that assumption, the value at any x between them is fixed by simple geometry: it's the same fraction of the way from y1 to y2 as x is from x1 to x2.

Deriving the formula

Call that fraction t — how far across the gap in x our query point sits, from 0 (at x1) to 1 (at x2): 

t = (x - x1) / (x2 - x1)

Because the line has constant slope, the same fraction t applies to the y side of the gap. Starting at y1 and moving t of the way to y2 gives: 

y = y1 + t * (y2 - y1)

This is the same line written in point-slope form — the slope (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1) multiplied by the distance from x1, added to the starting height y1. Two equivalent ways of saying the same thing.

A worked example

Suppose you know y = 10 at x = 0 and y = 30 at x = 10, and you want the value at x = 4: 

t = (4 - 0) / (10 - 0) = 0.4
y = 10 + 0.4 * (30 - 10) = 10 + 8 = 18

x = 4 sits 40% of the way from 0 to 10, so the estimate sits 40% of the way from 10 to 30. That's all linear interpolation ever does — it never looks at any point beyond the pair bracketing the query.

When the straight line is wrong

Linear interpolation is only exact if the true relationship between x and y really is a straight line between those two samples. For anything curved — acceleration, growth, decay — a straight line between two samples will systematically miss the true curve in between, and the error grows with how far apart the two known points are and how much the real function bends between them. Methods that fit a curve instead of a straight segment — a quadratic through three points, a cubic spline through a whole series, matching a curve's slope as well as its value at each sample — trade that error for more assumptions about how smoothly the data behaves. None of them are "more correct"; they're different bets about the shape of what you didn't measure.

Interpolation vs. extrapolation

Interpolation only makes sense strictly between two known points. Estimating past the last known point isn't interpolation anymore — it's extrapolation, and it's a fundamentally different kind of guess.

Inside the known range, a value is pinned down by data on both sides — the line (or curve) is constrained at both ends. Outside that range, nothing pins the estimate down at all: infinitely many different curves pass through the same known points but diverge the moment you step past them, and nothing in the data tells you which one is right. Projecting the last line segment onward is just one arbitrary choice among all of those — a straight-line guess dressed up as a calculation. That's why interpolation is only ever defined for x values that fall between two points you actually know.
